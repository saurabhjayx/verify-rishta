# Maa Chandraghanta: Why Is the Goddess Shown with a Bell-Shaped Moon?

Maa Chandraghanta is worshipped on the third day in the widely followed Navadurga sequence. Her name refers to the bell-shaped crescent moon associated with her forehead. Her iconography often shows a warrior Goddess with many arms, riding a lion or tiger, although artistic depictions vary.

## Why a bell?

The bell is a striking image because it can suggest both sound and alertness. In devotional explanations, Chandraghanta is associated with courage, readiness and protection. Her fierce appearance does not mean anger for its own sake; it communicates the power to confront danger and defend what is right.

## One form, several stories

Some retellings connect this form with Parvati after her marriage to Shiva, while other popular summaries focus on her warrior aspect. Mythological stories develop through many texts, oral traditions and regional retellings. No short version online is the only accepted story.

## How the day is observed

On the third day, devotees may recite prayers, offer flowers or follow local puja practices. Some traditions connect particular colours or offerings with the day, but these vary. If you follow a [daily colour chart](/navratri/navratri-colours-meaning-and-tradition), check which year and tradition it is based on.

## Courage, not aggression

Her imagery invites a distinction between aggression and courage. Courage is not the absence of fear; it is the ability to act with clarity when something important needs protection. That is a modern reflection inspired by the symbolism, not a promised religious result.

## The bottom line

The third night brings a new quality into the Navratri sequence: alertness and action. After the grounding of [Shailputri](/navratri/maa-shailputri-navratri-day-1) and the discipline of [Brahmacharini](/navratri/maa-brahmacharini-navratri-day-2), Chandraghanta stands for the readiness to face a challenge.
