# What Should Couples Verify Before Marriage, and What Should They Discuss Together?

A strong decision before marriage needs two kinds of clarity: whether important factual claims are accurate, and whether both people want a compatible future. Verification can help with selected facts. It cannot tell you whether two people will communicate well, share responsibilities fairly, or agree on how to raise children. Both parts matter.

## Part 1: Facts worth verifying

Depending on your circumstances, consider identity and age; education and professional qualifications; employment and income claims that affect shared plans; current location and relocation expectations; marital status and relevant previous-marriage disclosures; and financial obligations that both people agree to discuss. Use proportionate, lawful methods and obtain consent where required. Keep a record of what is confirmed, self-reported, inconclusive or not checked.

## Part 2: Questions worth discussing

- **Children:** Do we want children, and if so, when?
- **Careers:** Will both partners continue working, and how will relocation or career breaks be handled?
- **Money:** How will we budget, save, manage debt and support relatives?
- **Family:** What boundaries and caregiving responsibilities do we expect?
- **Home life:** How will chores and mental load be shared?
- **Lifestyle:** What are our expectations around religion or personal values, social life, travel, health routines and time alone?
- **Conflict:** How do we handle disagreement, apologies and decisions?

These are conversations, not items that a document can verify.

## Make the conversations concrete

Avoid relying only on broad questions such as "Are you family-oriented?" Ask for examples: "If one of us receives a job offer in another city, how should we decide?" or "How would we divide household responsibilities during a busy work period?" Specific scenarios help reveal expectations that abstract answers can hide.

## Build a shared checklist

For each topic, write down the question, each person's current view, areas of agreement, unresolved differences and the next step. You do not need to agree on everything immediately. You do need to understand which differences are negotiable and which are fundamental. Consider premarital counselling if conversations become difficult or recurring disagreements remain unresolved.

## Use verification without replacing trust

A respectful process is mutual and transparent. Tell each other what you want to verify and why; avoid secret surveillance; protect sensitive documents; and give each person a chance to explain discrepancies. A clean report does not guarantee compatibility, and a disagreement about future plans does not automatically mean someone has misrepresented facts.

## What the law says

The provisions below are summarised from the official text on India Code. Laws and their commencement change, so check the current text or ask a lawyer before you rely on them.

- [Section 6 of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023](https://indiacode.gov.in/act/c058fa9f-eaf0-4ca3-98f1-3443b087bca9/sections) says consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, and limited to the personal data needed for the stated purpose.
- [Section 3 of the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961](https://indiacode.gov.in/act/8dc8e1b4-7147-45ea-9e82-5911aa3816d4/sections) penalises giving or taking dowry, and [Section 4](https://indiacode.gov.in/act/8dc8e1b4-7147-45ea-9e82-5911aa3816d4/sections) penalises demanding it, directly or indirectly, from the family of the bride or groom.

## The bottom line

Know what can be checked, discuss what must be understood, and leave room for both people to make a free and informed choice. Verification supports that decision; it does not make it for you.
